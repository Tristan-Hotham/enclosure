Between the 1750s and the 1830s Parliament passed thousands of Acts that fenced off England's open fields and commons. I took every one listed in the official chronological tables, {{source.n_all|int}} of them, and put the parishes they name on a map, year by year.

Picture a Midland village around 1750. There are no hedged fields. Instead two or three huge open fields stretch away from the houses, each divided into long narrow strips, and every farmer's strips are scattered across all of them. Beyond the fields lie the commons and wastes: rough grazing, heath, marsh and wood, where villagers with common rights could keep a cow or geese, cut turf and gather fuel. A century later almost all of it had gone, replaced by the patchwork of hedged fields that people now think of as timeless English countryside.

Much of that change was made by Act of Parliament. Where the landowners of a parish could not all agree to enclose, the big proprietors could petition for a private Act, which appointed commissioners to survey the parish, extinguish common rights and share the land out. The legislation.gov.uk website publishes Parliament's own list of these Acts, its Chronological Tables of Private and of Local Acts. I extracted every title that mentions inclosure, the old spelling: {{source.n_private|int}} private Acts and {{source.n_local|int}} local Acts that enclosed land in England and Wales. Then I split them into the {{geo.places|int}} parishes and townships they name and found {{geo.matched|int}} of them on the map.

Two waves

Enclosure by Act came in two great waves. The first built up in the 1750s and peaked in the 1760s and 1770s: {{peaks.y1760_79|int}} private Acts in twenty years, with {{peaks.first_wave_n|int}} in {{peaks.first_wave_year|raw}} alone. The 1780s were quieter. Then came the wars with Revolutionary and Napoleonic France, from {{meta.war.0|raw}} to {{meta.war.1|raw}}. Harvests failed, imports were disrupted and wheat prices soared, and suddenly it paid to plough up commons and rearrange open fields. In those {{eras.war_years|int}} years Parliament passed {{peaks.y1793_1815_all|int}} enclosure Acts, {{eras.all.war_pct|int}}% of all those in the tables, with a peak of {{peaks.all_n|int}} in {{peaks.all_year|raw}}. When peace came, the boom stopped dead: {{peaks.y1814|int}} Acts in 1814, {{peaks.y1816|int}} in 1816.

{{IMG|b_years.png|Bar chart of enclosure Acts per year from 1700 to 1849. Almost nothing before 1750, then a first wave of green bars of around 50 to 87 a year in the 1760s and 1770s, peaking at 87 in 1777, a dip in the 1780s, and a second, larger wave in the shaded war years 1793 to 1815, where gold local Acts stack on the green private ones to reach 125 in 1811, before a sharp fall after 1815|Enclosure Acts per year: private Acts in green, local Acts in gold, the French wars shaded.}}

There is a twist in the records. From {{peaks.local_first|raw}} more and more enclosure Acts were listed in the table of local Acts instead of the private one: in 1811 to 1814 about half of them. Look at the private Acts alone and the second wave seems to peak in {{peaks.private_year|raw}} and fade. Add the two tables together and it keeps rising until 1811.

The Midland belt

Enclosure by Act was above all a Midland business. Northamptonshire had more Acts for its size than any other county: {{counties.Northamptonshire.private|int}} private Acts, {{counties.Northamptonshire.per100_private|f1}} for every 100 square kilometres. Leicestershire, Rutland, Huntingdonshire, Nottinghamshire and Warwickshire follow. Twelve Midland and East Midland counties, about a fifth of England by area, account for {{belt.acts_pct|int}}% of its private enclosure Acts. This was the heartland of the open-field system, where it had survived longest.

At the other end of the list are counties that hardly needed Parliament, because they had been enclosed piece by piece or by private agreement long before, or had never had much open field. Kent had {{counties.Kent.private|int}} private enclosure Acts, Devon {{counties.Devon.private|int}}, and Cornwall exactly {{counties.Cornwall.private|int}}. Yorkshire had the most of any county by number, {{counties.Yorkshire.private|int}}, but it is enormous; for its size it sits in the middle of the table.

The second wave went somewhere new

The war-years boom was not a repeat of the first wave. In 1750 to 1779 more than half the Acts were for the Midland belt; in the war years, under a third. The new ground was elsewhere. Norfolk and Suffolk went from {{waves.w1.eastanglia_pct|int}}% of all Acts to {{waves.war.eastanglia_pct|int}}%, the south-west and the Welsh border from {{waves.w1.southwest_pct|int}}% to {{waves.war.southwest_pct|int}}%, and Wales, almost untouched before, began to enclose its hill commons. Norfolk, with its light sandy heaths, ended up with {{counties.Norfolk.all|int}} Acts, {{counties.Norfolk.war_pct|int}}% of them in the war years. The north, by contrast, did not grow its share.

On the right, blue dots for the war years of 1793 to 1815 spread much more widely, densest in Norfolk and Suffolk and across the south and west, with scattered dots in Wales. Average the position of each decade's Acts and you get a centre of gravity that wanders, but never far. In the 1760s it sat on the edge of Nottingham, pulled north by Yorkshire and Lincolnshire. By the 1810s it had moved about {{centroid_move.km|int}} km, to the country west of Leicester. It never left the East Midlands.

Odd ones

The very first title in the tables that mentions enclosure is from {{first.year|raw}}, under Elizabeth I: an Act for draining and enclosing Plumstead Marsh, on the Thames below London. The biggest is a {{biggest.year|raw}} Act naming {{biggest.n_places|int}} places around Shenstone in Staffordshire. And some places come up again and again. {{twice.n|int}} are named in enclosure Acts of two or more different years, usually because a parish enclosed its open fields first and its commons later. Elsdon in Northumberland appears in four, from 1728 to 1807. At Eccleshall in Staffordshire, a heath was enclosed in 1719 and the parish again in 1841, {{twice_gap.0.gap|int}} years later.

Who won, who lost

Historians have argued about enclosure for over a century, and both sides have a point. Enclosed land could be drained, consolidated and farmed with new crops and rotations, and studies such as J. D. Chambers and G. E. Mingay's The Agricultural Revolution and Michael Turner's English Parliamentary Enclosure found that output and rents rose where it happened. Robert Allen's Enclosure and the Yeoman argued that the gains were smaller than claimed and went mostly to landlords. On the other side, the Hammonds' The Village Labourer and J. M. Neeson's Commoners showed what the poor lost: the right to graze a cow, gather fuel and glean, which had kept many families afloat. Those who could prove a legal claim got land; many cottagers got nothing, or a scrap too small to use. More food from the same land, and much of it taken from the people with least: both can be true.

How I did this

The source is legislation.gov.uk's Chronological Tables of Private and of Local Acts (Open Government Licence). I rejoined titles split over two rows, dropped the notes recording later repeals, dated each Act by its parliamentary session, and kept every title mentioning inclosure, setting aside amendments and Acts for Ireland. Places were matched to a Wikidata gazetteer within the county the title names, allowing for old spellings; {{geo.rate|int}}% were found, and nothing else was guessed. Two pages of the local table, for 1802 to 1803 and for 1841, could not be collected. This counts Acts as the tables list them, not every enclosure: the figure of more than 5,000 English enclosure Acts that historians usually quote includes Acts and awards these tables do not itemise, and much enclosure happened by agreement without any Act. County shapes are rebuilt from the gazetteer. The paper sets out every step.
